Nexventory Algorithms and Data Structures

A practical restock priority system for an Android inventory application.

The artifact and its purpose

Nexventory is an Android inventory application I began in CS 360. My experience with Army inventory management gave me a practical reason to build a clearer way to organize supplies. For CS 499, I enhanced the application to help users decide which items need restocking first. This page presents the algorithms and data structures enhancement, completed in September 2026, building on the earlier software design improvements.

I selected this artifact because it connects my interest in networking with a useful computing problem. Cables, adapters, and switches may have different stock targets. A list of quantities alone does not explain what needs attention. The enhancement turns those records into a repeatable priority list.

What changed

Each item now has a minimum-stock target. Its shortage is the minimum minus the available quantity, with negative results treated as zero. Items at their target are excluded from restocking. A target of zero disables automatic restocking, even when the physical quantity is zero.

Eligible items with no stock appear first. Within that group and the remaining candidates, larger shortages come first. Equal shortages are resolved by product name and then the saved item ID. For example, an Adapter with quantity zero and minimum four appears before a Patch Cable with quantity three and minimum ten. The adapter has no available stock, even though the cable has a larger shortage. Stock filters and name or quantity sorting help users inspect the same inventory from different perspectives.

Algorithm choices and evidence

The production implementation filters records into an ArrayList and sorts them with a Comparator. Filtering n records takes O(n) time; sorting k matching records takes O(k log k) time and uses O(k) additional storage. I also compared a PriorityQueue that is filled and then drained. Both approaches have O(n + k log k) time for a complete ordered display. The sorted list is simpler for this interface; a queue would be useful for repeatedly retrieving only the next urgent item.

The saved verification record reports 59,321 assertions across boundary cases, ordering rules, comparator properties, and 100 randomized inventories. This is an assertion count, not a count of distinct tests. On the recorded desktop benchmark, 100,000 records produced 49,509 candidates. Median times were approximately 16.27 milliseconds for the list and 18.39 milliseconds for the drained queue. These measurements cover in-memory ranking on that computer, not Android screen or database performance.

The recorded Android Studio unit and connected test tasks completed successfully. Manual emulator checks covered target persistence, restock ordering, filters, search combined with restocking, invalid minimums, and cancellation of deletion. The linked verification notes distinguish automated, desktop, and user-confirmed Android evidence.

Learning and challenges

The main lesson was to define the stock rule before choosing the data structure. A fixed low-stock threshold did not account for the needs of each item. Keeping the new rule consistent across cards, counts, and optional alerts required care. The zero-target case helped me recognize that being physically out of stock and needing an automatic reorder are separate conditions.

The instructor awarded the algorithms milestone 40/40 and described the coding as sound and the logic as positioned for future enhancements. No corrections were requested in that feedback. This publication preserves the graded algorithm and clarifies its decisions and limits. Future work could consider equipment criticality, supplier lead times, and a more efficient display for large inventories.
